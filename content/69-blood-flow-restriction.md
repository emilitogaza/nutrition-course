# Blood-Flow-Restriction (BFR) Training

Everything in the hypertrophy chapters points to one conclusion: to build muscle you
need either **heavy loads** or **light loads taken close to failure**, and mechanical
tension is the currency. Blood-flow-restriction training looks, at first glance, like
it breaks that rule — people build meaningful muscle lifting **20–30% of their
one-rep max**, a load so light it would normally do almost nothing.

It doesn't break the rule. It's a clever way of reaching the same end — high
motor-unit recruitment and metabolic stress — with a fraction of the joint and
tissue load. That's why it started in rehabilitation and has stayed most useful
there.

## What it is

BFR — also called **occlusion training** or by the brand name **KAATSU**, where it
originated in Japan — means applying a **cuff or band** around the top of a limb
(upper arm or upper thigh) to **partially restrict blood flow** during light-load
exercise. The cuff:

- **Reduces arterial inflow** somewhat, and
- **Occludes venous outflow** more completely, so blood pools in the working muscle.

You then do **high-rep sets with light weights**. The limb fills with blood, the
muscle burns intensely, and you reach failure far sooner than the light load would
otherwise allow.

The key word is **partial**. This is not a tourniquet and it is not meant to fully
cut off circulation. Done right it's uncomfortable, not dangerous.

## Why light loads suddenly work

Under normal conditions, light loads recruit only your smaller, fatigue-resistant
**type I** motor units, and you stop the set long before the big **type II**
(growth-prone) fibres get involved. BFR changes that:

- Restricting blood flow makes the muscle fatigue **much faster**, creating a
  low-oxygen, high-metabolite environment.
- As the type I fibres fatigue early, the nervous system is forced to **recruit the
  larger type II motor units** to keep the muscle contracting — the same high
  recruitment you'd normally need heavy weight to achieve.
- The accumulation of **metabolites** and **cell swelling** adds anabolic signalling
  on top.

The net effect: recruitment and growth signalling that resemble heavy training, at a
load the joints, tendons, and healing tissues barely notice.

## What the evidence shows

The research base is now substantial, and reasonably consistent:

- **Low-load BFR builds muscle** far better than the same light load without a cuff,
  and — for **hypertrophy** — approaches (though usually doesn't quite beat)
  conventional heavy training.
- For **maximal strength**, heavy lifting still wins. BFR grows the muscle; it
  doesn't train the nervous system to express force under a heavy bar the way heavy
  lifting does. If your goal is a bigger squat, BFR is a supplement, not a
  replacement.
- Its standout evidence is in **rehabilitation and unloading**: in people recovering
  from surgery or injury, and in limbs that can't tolerate heavy loads, BFR
  **preserves or builds muscle and strength** that would otherwise waste away. There's
  even evidence that **passive BFR** (cuff without exercise) can slow muscle loss
  during immobilisation.

## Where it genuinely earns its place

BFR is a **tool for specific situations**, not a general replacement for lifting
heavy. It shines when you can't, or shouldn't, load a joint hard:

- **Rehab after injury or surgery.** The headline use. When a knee or shoulder can't
  take heavy load yet, BFR lets you keep building the muscle around it — bridging the
  gap until heavy training returns. Connects directly to the tissue-healing chapter:
  loading is medicine, and BFR is a way to load when you otherwise can't.
- **Working around a cranky joint.** Achy elbows, knees, or a tweaked back (see the
  injury chapters) often tolerate light-load BFR when heavy work flares them up.
- **Deloads and high-frequency phases.** Getting a hypertrophy stimulus with minimal
  systemic and joint fatigue — useful when you want to keep stimulating a muscle
  without accumulating strain.
- **Adding volume to lagging muscles** without piling on more heavy, joint-taxing
  work — arms and calves are popular targets.
- **When you have limited equipment** — a band and light dumbbells can produce a
  real stimulus.

For a healthy lifter with no limitations, it's an **optional accessory**, not a
priority. Heavy compound lifting remains the main course.

## How to actually do it

The parameters are well established. Precision on the **pressure** and the **rep
scheme** matters more than anything.

**Cuff placement:** high on the limb — upper arm (for arms) or upper thigh (for
legs). BFR works only for **limbs**; you can't apply it to the torso, so it's not a
tool for chest, back, or core.

**Pressure:** enough to restrict, not to occlude. The evidence-based way to set it is
relative to your **arterial occlusion pressure (AOP)** — the pressure that fully
stops flow:

- **Arms: ~40–50% of AOP.** **Legs: ~50–80% of AOP.**
- With practical wraps that don't measure AOP, aim for a **perceived tightness of
  about 7 out of 10** — clearly tight, never numb, tingling, or painful. You should
  keep full sensation and be able to release it instantly.

**Reps and load:** light load, high reps, short rest — the classic scheme:

| Variable | Prescription |
| --- | --- |
| **Load** | **20–40% of 1RM** (often ~30%) |
| **Rep scheme** | **30, then 15, 15, 15** across 4 sets |
| **Rest** | **30–60 s** between sets (short — the point is to keep blood pooled) |
| **Cuff** | Stays on through all four sets, released *after* the last one |
| **Frequency** | Can be done more often than heavy training — 2–4×/week per area |
| **Time under restriction** | Keep total occlusion per bout **under ~15–20 minutes** |

You'll know it's working: the last sets burn hard and reaching failure feels close.
Take sets close to failure, as with any light-load hypertrophy work.

## Safety and who should be careful

For healthy people, correctly applied BFR has a **strong safety record** — the
cardiovascular stress is comparable to or lower than heavy lifting, and serious
adverse events in the literature are rare. But it deserves respect:

- **Never make it a tourniquet.** Partial restriction only. Numbness, tingling,
  colour changes, or sharp pain mean **stop and release immediately**.
- **Use proper cuffs where you can.** Purpose-made BFR cuffs (ideally with pressure
  measurement) are safer and more consistent than improvised elastic wraps cranked
  arbitrarily tight.
- **Blood-clotting risk is the main contraindication.** Anyone with a history of
  **DVT, clotting disorders, or on anticoagulants** should avoid it unless cleared
  by a doctor. Also caution with **uncontrolled hypertension, cardiovascular
  disease, pregnancy, varicose veins, or sickle cell trait**.
- **In rehab, do it under guidance.** Post-surgical BFR should be programmed by a
  **physiotherapist (fysioterapeut)** — both for the pressure settings and for the
  medical clearance. This is where its value is highest and where getting it right
  matters most.

> **Not a substitute for medical advice.** If you're using BFR to rehab an injury or
> around a medical condition, set it up with a physiotherapist rather than a YouTube
> video.

## The takeaway

- BFR uses a **cuff to partially restrict blood flow** during **light-load** exercise
  (20–40% 1RM), building muscle nearly as well as heavy lifting at a fraction of the
  joint load.
- It works by forcing early fatigue, which **recruits the big type II fibres** you'd
  normally need heavy weight to reach.
- For **hypertrophy** it rivals heavy training; for **maximal strength**, heavy
  lifting still wins — BFR is a supplement, not a replacement.
- Its **best-evidenced use is rehab and unloading** — keeping muscle when a joint
  can't take heavy load — plus deloads, cranky joints, and lagging muscles.
- Dose it: **~30% 1RM, a 30/15/15/15 scheme, 30–60 s rest**, cuff at **~40–50% AOP
  (arms) / 50–80% (legs)** or a **7/10 tightness** — never numb or painful.
- **Contraindicated with clotting risk** and several cardiovascular conditions; for
  rehab, program it with a **physiotherapist**.
