# Heart Health: Blood Lipids, Cholesterol & Cardiovascular Risk

This course has spent a lot of time on how food changes how you look and perform.
This chapter is about how it changes **how long you live** — because
**cardiovascular disease is still the leading cause of death in Sweden and the
world**, and it's the outcome most influenced by the things you already control:
what you eat, how much you train, and how much body fat you carry.

The dietary-fat and omega-3 chapters covered the fats themselves. This one is about
the **markers in your blood** — what they mean, which ones actually predict risk,
and how to move them. It's also where a lot of internet nutrition goes badly wrong,
so it's worth getting the real picture.

> **Education, not medical advice.** If your numbers are abnormal, or you have a
> family history of early heart disease, this chapter is to help you have a better
> conversation at the vårdcentral — not to replace one.

## Cholesterol isn't the villain — it's the delivery trucks

Cholesterol itself is essential: it builds cell membranes, hormones, and bile. The
problem was never cholesterol existing; it's how much is being **shipped around your
bloodstream** and getting lodged in artery walls.

Cholesterol doesn't dissolve in blood, so it travels inside protein-wrapped
particles called **lipoproteins**. The standard blood test reports the cholesterol
*carried by* each type:

| Marker | What it is | The short version |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **LDL cholesterol** | Cholesterol in low-density lipoproteins | The particles that **deposit cholesterol into artery walls**. "Bad" — genuinely the causal driver. Lower is better. |
| **HDL cholesterol** | Cholesterol in high-density lipoproteins | Associated with **removing** cholesterol. Higher tends to be better, but raising it with drugs hasn't reduced risk — so it's a *marker*, not a lever. |
| **Triglycerides** | Fat circulating in the blood | High levels track with **insulin resistance, excess calories, alcohol, and visceral fat**. Very responsive to lifestyle. |
| **Total cholesterol** | LDL + HDL + a fraction of triglycerides | Too crude on its own — a high HDL can inflate it harmlessly. Don't judge by this number alone. |
| **Non-HDL cholesterol** | Total minus HDL | Everything atherogenic in one number. Better than LDL alone. |
| **ApoB** | A count of the atherogenic particles themselves | The best single marker — see below. |

## LDL and ApoB: the causal story, honestly

Here is where you have to separate genuine scientific debate from internet
contrarianism. The claim that **LDL/ApoB causally drives atherosclerosis** is one of
the most robustly supported findings in all of medicine — it's backed by:

- **Genetics** (people with lifelong low LDL from gene variants have very low heart
  disease; those with **familial hypercholesterolaemia** and lifelong high LDL have
  very high risk),
- **Every class of LDL-lowering drug** independently reducing events in proportion to
  how much it lowers LDL, and
- Decades of consistent epidemiology.

This is a settled causal relationship, not a hypothesis. The nuance worth keeping is
about **magnitude and context**, not direction: LDL is one causal factor among
several (blood pressure, smoking, diabetes, and inflammation all matter), and an
individual's *overall* risk depends on the whole picture.

**ApoB** is the sharpening of the story. Each atherogenic particle (LDL, VLDL,
and others) carries exactly **one ApoB protein**, so measuring ApoB **counts the
particles** rather than the cholesterol inside them. Because it's the particle
number that drives arterial damage, ApoB predicts risk better than LDL cholesterol —
especially in the common case where someone has lots of small, cholesterol-poor
particles (typical of insulin resistance) and a "normal-looking" LDL. If you can get
it measured, **ApoB is the number to watch.**

## Triglycerides: the lifestyle mirror

Where LDL is strongly genetic, **triglycerides** are the marker that moves most with
how you're living:

- They rise with **excess calories, refined carbohydrate and sugar, alcohol, and
  visceral fat**, and they fall fast with weight loss, training, and cutting alcohol.
- The **triglyceride-to-HDL** pattern (high trigs, low HDL) is a fingerprint of
  **insulin resistance** — the same metabolic dysfunction behind type 2 diabetes.
- Omega-3 (EPA/DHA) at higher doses lowers triglycerides more reliably than almost
  any lifestyle change (see the omega-3 chapter).

If your LDL is fine but your triglycerides are high and HDL low, the message usually
isn't "eat less fat" — it's "there's too much visceral fat and too little training."

## The saturated fat question, settled honestly

The internet has convinced a lot of people that saturated fat was "exonerated." It
wasn't — but the real position is more nuanced than either camp claims:

- Saturated fat **raises LDL** in most people, and replacing it with **unsaturated
  fat** (olive oil, rapeseed oil, nuts, fatty fish) **lowers LDL and cardiovascular
  events**. This replacement is one of the better-supported dietary moves for heart
  health.
- The confusion came from studies replacing saturated fat with **refined
  carbohydrate** — which does *not* help, because you've swapped one problem for
  another. **What you replace it with is the whole question.**
- Whole-food sources matter too: the "food matrix" of, say, cheese or yoghurt
  behaves differently from butter or processed meat. It's a spectrum, not a switch.

The practical read: you don't need to fear fat, but the old advice — **more
unsaturated, less saturated, and mostly from whole foods** — has held up. This lines
up exactly with the **Nordic Nutrition Recommendations 2023** and the Mediterranean/
Nordic dietary patterns discussed in the diet-frameworks chapter.

## What actually lowers cardiovascular risk

The levers, roughly in order of how much they move the needle for most people:

1. **Don't smoke.** Nothing else in this chapter competes.
2. **Build cardiorespiratory fitness.** Low fitness is among the strongest
   predictors of mortality there is (see the VO₂ max chapter) — this is where
   endurance training earns its keep for the heart, not just the legs.
3. **Lose excess visceral fat.** It improves triglycerides, blood pressure, blood
   sugar, and inflammation together.
4. **Fix the diet pattern:** more fibre (the *Lancet* 2019 data on mortality lives in
   the fibre chapter), more unsaturated fat, more plants and fish, less refined
   carbohydrate and alcohol.
5. **Control blood pressure** — the other giant, partly diet-driven (sodium,
   potassium, weight, alcohol, fitness).
6. **Lower LDL/ApoB** — via the above, and with **medication** when risk is high
   enough. Which brings us to statins.

## A word on statins and medication

At some level of risk, lifestyle isn't enough on its own, and this is where a lot of
online advice does real harm by discouraging effective treatment.

- **Statins** lower LDL and reduce heart attacks and strokes, with a strong
  evidence base. The side effects are real but **far less common** than internet
  forums suggest (much of the reported muscle ache doesn't reproduce when tested
  blind against placebo).
- Whether *you* need one isn't about LDL alone — it's about your **overall risk**,
  which a clinician estimates using tools like **SCORE2** (the European risk chart)
  plus your age, blood pressure, smoking, and family history.
- **Familial hypercholesterolaemia (FH)** — genetically very high LDL from birth,
  affecting roughly 1 in 250 people — is dangerously **underdiagnosed** and is a
  case where medication early genuinely saves lives. A personal or family history of
  heart attacks before ~55 (men) or ~65 (women) is a flag to get checked.

Training hard does not make you immune. Fit, lean people still have heart attacks,
usually when a genetic lipid problem went unmeasured. **Knowing your numbers is the
point** — which is the next chapter.

## The takeaway

- **Cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of death**, and it's heavily
  influenced by the levers you already control.
- Cholesterol travels in particles: **LDL deposits it in arteries** (the causal "bad"
  one), HDL is a marker not a lever, and **triglycerides mirror your lifestyle and
  visceral fat**.
- That **LDL/ApoB causally drives heart disease is settled** — backed by genetics and
  every LDL-lowering drug. **ApoB counts the harmful particles** and is the best
  single marker if you can get it.
- Saturated fat **raises LDL**; replacing it with **unsaturated fat lowers risk** —
  replacing it with refined carbs doesn't. The classic advice held up.
- Biggest levers: **don't smoke, build fitness, lose visceral fat, eat more fibre and
  unsaturated fat, control blood pressure** — and **lower LDL/ApoB**, with a statin
  when overall risk warrants it.
- **Familial hypercholesterolaemia is common and underdiagnosed** — early heart
  disease in the family means get your lipids checked.
