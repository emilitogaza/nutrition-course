# Supplement Safety & Anti-Doping

Most of this section has been about whether supplements *work*. This chapter is
about the risk that has nothing to do with whether they work: **what's actually in
the bottle**, and what it can cost you.

For a recreational lifter the stakes are usually money and mild side effects. For a
**tested athlete** — anyone competing under a sports federation, from national
level down to surprisingly ordinary amateur competition — a contaminated scoop of
pre-workout can end a career. And the uncomfortable truth is that the label is not a
guarantee, because of how supplements are regulated.

## The regulation gap

Recall from the "supplements that don't work" chapter: supplements are **not**
tested for safety or efficacy before sale the way medicines are.

- **In Sweden and the EU**, most supplements are regulated as **food** by
  **Livsmedelsverket**, not as medicines by Läkemedelsverket. Food law governs
  hygiene and labelling — it does **not** require anyone to verify that the contents
  match the label before the product reaches a shelf.
- That means the responsibility for "is there actually 5 g of creatine in here, and
  *only* creatine?" falls on the manufacturer's own quality control — which ranges
  from excellent to nonexistent.
- Enforcement is **reactive**: a product gets pulled after a problem is found, not
  before it's sold.

None of this makes supplements dangerous by default. Reputable brands run tight
operations. But it does mean **the label is a claim, not a certificate** — and for
some people that gap matters enormously.

## How contamination actually happens

Independent testing programmes have repeatedly found that a meaningful fraction of
sports supplements contain something not on the label — often a banned substance.
The routes are mundane, not conspiratorial:

- **Cross-contamination in shared facilities.** A factory that makes a
  prohormone or a stimulant product and then makes your protein on the same line,
  with imperfect cleaning, can transfer trace amounts. Trace is enough to fail a
  test.
- **Cheap or adulterated raw ingredients.** Bulk powders sourced on price can carry
  undeclared stimulants, steroids, or drug analogues — this is most common in the
  categories built on "feeling something": **pre-workouts, fat burners, "test
  boosters," and weight-loss products.**
- **Deliberate spiking.** Some products are laced with unlabelled active drugs so
  they "work," which is both a doping risk and a genuine health hazard.
- **Botanicals with surprises.** Herbal products can naturally contain, or be
  contaminated with, prohibited compounds (e.g. higenamine, DMAA, DMHA turning up
  in "natural" fat burners).

The categories at highest risk map almost perfectly onto the categories with the
worst evidence. **The products least likely to help are the ones most likely to
contain a nasty surprise.** That is not a coincidence.

## Strict liability: why "I didn't know" is no defence

Anti-doping runs on a principle called **strict liability**: if a prohibited
substance is in your body, **you are responsible**, regardless of how it got there
or whether you intended it. "It was in my protein" is not, by itself, a defence — it
may reduce a sanction if you can *prove* the source, but the burden is on the
athlete, and proving it after the fact is extremely hard.

This is why supplement contamination isn't a footnote for competitive athletes. A
single failed test can mean a **multi-year ban**, and the substance responsible is
frequently something the athlete never knowingly took.

## Third-party testing: what it does and doesn't certify

The mitigation is **batch-level third-party testing**. Independent programmes test
supplement products (ideally every batch) for banned substances and certify the
ones that pass. The main marks:

| Programme | What to know |
| --- | --- |
| **Informed Sport** | Batch-by-batch testing against the WADA list; the most-recognised mark for tested athletes. Each tested batch is searchable online. |
| **Informed Choice** | Same lab, but tests a *sample* of batches rather than every one — a lower assurance level than Informed Sport. |
| **NSF Certified for Sport** | Widely used, especially in North America; also verifies label accuracy and manufacturing quality. |

Two things these marks **do** tell you:

- The tested batch was screened for a broad list of prohibited substances.
- (For NSF and Informed Sport) the product was made under decent quality controls.

And what they **don't** tell you:

- **Nothing about whether the supplement works.** Certification is a purity/safety
  check, not an efficacy check. A useless product can be perfectly clean.
- **Testing can't screen for every possible novel compound**, so "batch tested"
  reduces risk sharply but never to literal zero.

For a tested athlete the rule is simple: **if it isn't batch-tested, don't take it.**
The reduction in risk is worth the small price premium, and it narrows your choices
to serious manufacturers anyway.

## The short list that's low-risk anyway

The supplements this course actually recommends are, conveniently, the ones with the
lowest contamination risk when bought sensibly:

- **Creatine monohydrate**, **caffeine**, **whey/protein powder**, **vitamin D**,
  **omega-3**, **electrolytes** — single-ingredient, simple products from reputable
  EU brands, ideally batch-tested if you compete.
- The high-risk categories — **pre-workout blends, fat burners, "test boosters,"
  weight-loss and "natural anabolic" products** — are exactly the ones with the
  weakest evidence. Skipping them removes most of the risk for free.

## Anti-doping in Sweden

If you compete under a federation affiliated with **Riksidrottsförbundet (RF)**,
you're subject to the anti-doping rules administered by **Antidoping Sverige**
(Sweden's national anti-doping organisation), which enforces the **World Anti-Doping
Code** and the **WADA Prohibited List**.

Practical points:

- **The Prohibited List changes every year** (updated 1 January). Something
  permitted last season can be banned this one — always check the current list, not
  your memory. GLP-1 drugs, for example, are currently only *monitored*, not banned
  (see the GLP-1 chapter), and that could change.
- **"Ren vinnare"** is Antidoping Sverige's free education programme; federations
  increasingly require athletes to complete it. Worth doing even if you're not
  required to.
- **Check medicines, not just supplements.** Ordinary prescriptions and even some
  over-the-counter drugs contain prohibited substances (certain asthma, ADHD, and
  cold medicines). If you have a legitimate medical need for one, you apply for a
  **TUE (Therapeutic Use Exemption / dispens)** *in advance*.
- **Kolla Läkemedel** — the searchable tool for checking whether a specific
  medicine is prohibited under the current list — should be your habit before
  taking anything new.
- **Whereabouts and testing** apply to athletes in registered testing pools; if
  that's you, you already have formal guidance, and this chapter is the floor, not
  the ceiling.

## And the darker end: don't buy drugs as "supplements"

A recurring danger, separate from accidental contamination, is products that are
**deliberately** drugs dressed as supplements — **SARMs**, prohormones, "research
chemicals," peptides, and grey-market injectables sold online. These are not
supplements with a purity problem; they're **unapproved drugs** with unknown dosing,
real health risks (liver, cardiovascular, hormonal), and, for tested athletes, a
guaranteed ban. The fact that a website will sell it to you means nothing about its
legality or safety.

## The takeaway

- Supplements are regulated as **food** in Sweden (Livsmedelsverket), so the
  **label is a claim, not a verified certificate** — contents can differ from what's
  printed.
- **Contamination is real and recurring**, concentrated in the low-evidence
  categories: **pre-workouts, fat burners, "test boosters," weight-loss products.**
- Anti-doping runs on **strict liability** — "it was in my supplement" is your
  problem to prove, and rarely enough to avoid a ban.
- For tested athletes: **only use batch-tested products** (**Informed Sport**,
  **NSF Certified for Sport**), and remember these marks certify **purity, not
  efficacy**.
- In Sweden, **Antidoping Sverige** enforces the **WADA list** (updated every 1
  January); use **Kolla Läkemedel** for medicines and apply for a **TUE/dispens** in
  advance if you need a prohibited drug medically.
- The safest move is also the cheapest: **stick to the short list of simple,
  well-evidenced supplements** and skip the categories where risk and hype both live.
