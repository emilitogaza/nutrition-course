# Building Habits That Stick

Here is the uncomfortable truth about everything in this course: **knowing it is the
easy part.** Almost nobody fails at nutrition or training because they didn't know how
much protein to eat or how to structure a program. They fail because they couldn't
*keep doing* the thing they already knew how to do.

Adherence is the hidden variable behind every result. The "best diet" is the one you'll
actually follow; the best program is the one you'll actually show up for. A B-minus
plan executed for five years beats an A-plus plan abandoned in March. So this chapter
is about the real bottleneck — not more information, but the behavioural machinery that
turns intention into a default you don't have to think about.

## Why willpower is the wrong plan

The standard model of self-improvement is a motivation model: get inspired, grit your
teeth, force the new behaviour. It fails predictably, because **willpower is a poor,
finite, and unreliable resource**. It's high when you're rested and calm and
low — exactly when you need it most — when you're tired, stressed, hungry, or it's
raining and dark, which in Sweden is a lot of the year.

Motivation is real but it's a **spark, not an engine**. It's useful for starting; it's
useless for sustaining. Anything that depends on feeling motivated every day is built
on sand. The people who look disciplined from the outside usually aren't
white-knuckling it — they've **engineered their lives so the right choice requires
little willpower at all.** That engineering is the skill.

## How a habit actually forms

A habit is a behaviour that has become **automatic** — triggered by a context rather
than a decision. The well-established loop has three parts:

1. **Cue** — the trigger that starts it (a time, a place, a preceding action, an
   emotional state).
2. **Routine** — the behaviour itself.
3. **Reward** — the payoff that tells your brain "do that again."

Habits form through **repetition in a stable context**. Do the same thing, in response
to the same cue, enough times, and the deliberate decision fades into automaticity —
you stop choosing and start defaulting. The often-quoted "21 days" is a myth; the
research (Lally et al.) found it takes a **median of about 66 days**, ranging widely by
person and behaviour, for something to feel automatic. **Habits take a couple of
months to set, not a couple of weeks** — which is exactly why people quit in week
three, right before it would have gotten easier.

## The tactics that actually work

Decades of behaviour-change research point to a consistent, unglamorous toolkit.

**Start absurdly small.** The most common failure is starting too big. "Train an hour
every day" collapses; "put on my gym clothes" survives. A behaviour so small it feels
trivial gets **repeated**, and repetition — not intensity — is what builds the habit.
You can always do more once you're there; the win is establishing the *pattern*. Shrink
the first step until it's almost impossible to fail.

**Anchor new habits to existing ones (habit stacking).** The easiest cue is a behaviour
you already do reliably. "After I pour my morning coffee, I take my creatine and vitamin
D." "After I brush my teeth, I lay out my gym bag." You borrow the stability of an
established routine to prop up the new one.

**Design the environment — this is the highest-leverage tactic.** You do not rise to
your goals; you fall to your environment. Change the environment and the behaviour
follows with far less effort than willpower:

- **Make good choices easy and visible:** fruit in a bowl on the counter, protein at
  eye level in the fridge, prepped food ready to grab, the water bottle already
  filled, gym clothes laid out the night before.
- **Make bad choices hard and invisible:** don't keep the stuff you binge in the house.
  "I'll just have one" fails; "it's not in the flat and the shop is closed" succeeds.
  The kitchen you shop for on Sunday decides what you eat on Wednesday.
- **Reduce friction on the behaviour you want, add friction to the one you don't.**
  Every extra step matters more than it should.

**Use implementation intentions.** A specific *when-where-how* plan beats a vague goal.
Not "I'll train more" but "**I'll lift Monday, Wednesday and Friday at 17:30 at the gym
on my way home.**" The research on if-then planning is strong: deciding the details in
advance removes the in-the-moment negotiation where good intentions die.

**Track it — visibly.** A simple record (a calendar you mark, an app, a streak) makes
the behaviour real and provides its own small reward. "Don't break the chain" works
because the streak becomes something you don't want to lose. (The tracking chapter
covers the food-logging side — and its risks.)

**Plan for the miss in advance.** You *will* miss a workout, a day, a week. The people
who succeed aren't the ones who never slip — they're the ones who **treat a miss as a
data point, not a verdict.** The rule that matters: **never miss twice.** One missed
session is life; two in a row is a new habit forming in the wrong direction. Missing
once is inevitable; missing twice is a decision.

## The all-or-nothing trap

The single most destructive pattern in nutrition isn't a bad diet — it's the **binary
mindset**: "I've been good / I've blown it." One unplanned biscuit becomes "I've ruined
the day," which becomes "I'll start again Monday," which becomes a week undone by a
single cookie's worth of actual calories.

The maths exposes how irrational it is: **one meal is a rounding error** across a week,
but the *response* to that one meal — the write-off, the spiral, the four days of "screw
it" — is what actually derails progress. The skill is unremarkable consistency: a
so-so day is a so-so day, you carry on, and nothing is "ruined." Aiming for **"good
enough, most of the time"** beats chasing perfect and rebounding to nothing.

This is also where the line into disordered territory can blur. Rigid rules, guilt, and
all-or-nothing thinking are both bad for adherence *and* warning signs worth taking
seriously — the disordered-eating chapter is the necessary companion to this one.

## Identity: the deepest lever

The most durable behaviour change isn't about outcomes — it's about **identity**. There's
a difference between *"I'm trying to work out"* and *"I'm someone who trains."* The first
is a goal you're negotiating with; the second is just who you are, and people act in line
with how they see themselves.

Every time you perform the behaviour, you cast a small vote for that identity. Miss a
session and you haven't failed a goal — you've simply cast one vote the other way, and
you get to vote again tomorrow. Framing it this way does two useful things: it makes each
action feel meaningful, and it makes a slip feel survivable rather than disqualifying. The
aim isn't to *reach* a goal and stop; it's to *become* the kind of person for whom these
behaviours are simply normal.

## Motivation, sustainability, and the long game

A few closing principles that tie it together:

- **Consistency beats optimisation.** Perfect macros followed for three weeks lose to
  decent habits held for years. Almost everything in this course is subject to this
  rule — get the fundamentals consistent before you fine-tune.
- **Sustainability is a real criterion, not a cop-out.** If a plan requires conditions
  you can't maintain — no social life, endless prep time, permanent hunger — it will
  fail, and that's a design flaw in the plan, not a character flaw in you.
- **Sleep and stress set the ceiling.** Under-slept and overstressed, every good habit
  gets harder and every craving gets louder (see the sleep chapter). Protecting sleep
  is a behaviour-change intervention as much as a recovery one.
- **Self-compassion outperforms self-criticism.** The evidence is clear that beating
  yourself up predicts *giving up*, while treating a lapse kindly predicts getting back
  on track. Being your own harsh coach feels productive and isn't.

## A practical starting protocol

1. **Pick one habit**, not ten. The keystone ones — a protein floor, or lifting twice a
   week — tend to pull others along.
2. **Shrink it** until it's almost too easy, and **anchor it** to something you already
   do daily.
3. **Redesign your environment** so the right choice is the easy, visible, default one.
4. **Write an implementation intention:** when, where, how — specifically.
5. **Track it visibly**, and hold the rule **never miss twice.**
6. **Give it ~two months** to feel automatic before adding the next one.
7. **When you slip, note it and continue.** No write-offs, no "Monday."

## The takeaway

- **Knowledge isn't the bottleneck — adherence is.** The best plan is the one you'll
  actually keep doing. A B-minus plan for years beats an A-plus plan abandoned.
- **Willpower and motivation are sparks, not engines.** Don't build anything that
  depends on feeling motivated. Engineer the behaviour instead.
- Habits form by **repetition in a stable context**, and take a **median ~66 days** to
  feel automatic — not 21. Most people quit right before it gets easier.
- The evidence-based tactics: **start absurdly small, habit-stack onto existing
  routines, design your environment, use specific if-then plans, track visibly, and
  never miss twice.**
- Escape the **all-or-nothing trap**: one meal is a rounding error; the *write-off* is
  what does the damage. Aim for "good enough, most of the time."
- The deepest lever is **identity** — become "someone who trains," not someone chasing a
  goal — and **self-compassion beats self-criticism** for actually sticking with it.
