# Ketone Esters & Exogenous Ketones

Every few years endurance sport finds a new "secret weapon," and for most of the
last decade that weapon was **ketone esters**. They were the subject of Tour de
France rumours, a rare example of a supplement that started in a military research
lab, and — at their peak — the single most expensive thing an athlete could legally
drink. The reality, once the trials came in, is more interesting and less magical
than the hype.

This chapter sits in the supplements section rather than the "doesn't work" one for
a reason: exogenous ketones **do** something real. Whether that something is worth
the price is the actual question.

## The idea: a fourth fuel

Your body runs on three fuels — carbohydrate, fat, and (in a pinch) protein. Under
starvation or a **ketogenic diet**, the liver makes a fourth: **ketone bodies**
(mainly beta-hydroxybutyrate, "BHB") from fat, to keep the brain fuelled when
glucose is scarce.

Getting into that state naturally takes days of carbohydrate restriction — which,
for an athlete, means training on empty. **Exogenous ketones** are the shortcut:
drink BHB directly and blood ketone levels rise within 30 minutes, **without**
giving up carbohydrate. You get the ketone signal *on top of* a normal, fuelled
diet. That combination — high carbs **and** high ketones at once — never happens in
nature, which is part of why it's scientifically interesting.

The proposed benefits stack up nicely on paper:

- A **fuel that spares glycogen**, extending the tank in long events.
- A more **oxygen-efficient** fuel per unit of energy than fat or even glucose.
- A **post-exercise recovery signal** — ketones influence glycogen resynthesis,
  protein balance, and inflammation.

Paper is not the finish line.

## The three forms — and why most are useless

Not everything sold as "ketones" is the real thing.

| Form | What it is | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Ketone ester** | BHB bonded to a carrier (e.g. the "ketone monoester", (R)-BHB butanediol) | The form used in nearly all the serious research. Raises blood BHB highest. **Tastes vile.** Very expensive. |
| **Ketone salts** | BHB bound to sodium/potassium/calcium | Cheaper, common in shops. Raises ketones **far less**, and the mineral load gets high fast. Weak evidence. |
| **1,3-butanediol** | A precursor the liver converts to BHB | Slower, milder rise. Cheaper. Some newer products use it. |
| **"Raspberry ketones," MCT oil** | Unrelated to blood BHB | Not exogenous ketones at all. MCTs are a fat, sometimes marketed alongside. Ignore the label conflation. |

If a product is cheap and sold as "keto BHB capsules," it's almost certainly
**salts**, and it won't reproduce the ester research. The ester is the one that
moves blood ketones into the range studied — and it's the one that costs a fortune.

## What the performance trials actually found

This is where the story deflates. The first influential paper (**Cox et al., 2016**)
reported improved endurance performance with the monoester and generated most of the
excitement. Since then, a much larger body of controlled work has mostly **failed to
replicate an acute performance benefit**:

- Across time-trials and TT-style efforts, the **majority of studies show no
  improvement**, and several show a **small impairment** — partly because the ester
  can cause **GI distress** (nausea, gut discomfort) mid-effort, and partly because
  ketones can **blunt the very high power outputs** that depend on carbohydrate.
- The picture is best summarised as: exogenous ketones are **not an acute
  ergogenic aid** for most athletes in most events. If there's any performance
  signal, it's small, inconsistent, and easily outweighed by gut side effects.

Verdict on race-day performance: **not worth it.** Fuel with carbohydrate — the
best-evidenced endurance strategy there is (see the fuelling-around-training
chapter).

## Where the signal is more interesting: recovery and multi-day load

The more defensible use isn't during exercise — it's **after**, and especially
across **days of heavy accumulated load**.

- **Overreaching study (Poffé et al., 2019):** during a three-week block of
  intensified endurance training, athletes taking the ester after sessions showed
  **less of the overtraining signature** — better-maintained performance, lower
  night-time adrenaline, less overreaching — than placebo. The mechanism is thought
  to be ketones' effect on recovery and appetite/energy balance during heavy load.
- **Glycogen and protein signalling:** post-exercise, ketones may modestly support
  glycogen resynthesis and muscle protein synthesis, though carbohydrate + protein
  remains the foundation.

This is a genuinely more promising lane: not "drink this to win the sprint," but
"a recovery tool for athletes surviving a brutal training camp or a grand tour."
That is, notably, exactly the context the Tour de France rumours came from — teams
were reportedly using it to **recover between stages**, not to attack on the climbs.

## The other frontier: the brain and clinical use

Beyond sport, BHB is a signalling molecule as much as a fuel, and the interesting
research has drifted toward:

- **Cognition under stress** — like creatine, some work suggests ketones support
  brain energetics when glucose delivery is compromised.
- **Clinical settings** — heart failure, neurodegenerative disease, and metabolic
  conditions, where the brain or heart's ability to use glucose is impaired. This
  is real research, but it's **medical**, not a reason for a healthy lifter to buy
  ketones.

## Cost — the part that ends most conversations

There is no polite way to put this: ketone esters are **absurdly expensive**.

| Product | Rough cost | Per serving |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ketone **monoester** (research-grade brands) | — | **~30–60 kr per serving**, sometimes more |
| Ketone **salts** | cheaper | ~10–20 kr per serving, but far weaker effect |

At **30–60 kronor a dose**, used daily through a training block, the ester runs to
**hundreds of kronor a week** — for a supplement with no reliable acute performance
benefit and a recovery benefit that only clearly shows up under extreme load. For
almost everyone reading this, that money buys far more performance as **real food,
carbohydrate for long sessions, and sleep**.

## For tested athletes

Ketone esters and salts are **not on the WADA Prohibited List** — they're permitted
in and out of competition. As always with supplements, the risk isn't the ketones
themselves but **contamination** of a lightly regulated product; if you're tested,
choose a batch-tested brand (see the supplement safety & anti-doping chapter).

## Who, if anyone, should bother

- **Elite endurance athletes** in a **multi-day, high-load** block (stage racing,
  a heavy camp) who have already maximised everything cheaper — and can afford it.
  A defensible experiment.
- **Everyone else:** no. The foundations — carbohydrate periodisation, sleep,
  protein, and consistency — return vastly more per krona.

## The takeaway

- Exogenous ketones deliver a **fourth fuel** (BHB) without a ketogenic diet — a
  real physiological effect, and a genuinely novel one.
- The **ester** is the only form that reproduces the research; **salts** are cheaper
  and much weaker, and "keto BHB capsules" won't do what the studies did.
- For **acute performance**, the controlled trials are mostly **negative** — no
  reliable benefit, and often GI distress. Fuel with carbohydrate instead.
- The **stronger case is recovery under heavy multi-day load** (the overreaching
  research, and the actual Tour de France use case) — but that's an elite niche.
- At **~30–60 kr per serving**, it's the most expensive thing in endurance
  nutrition, and the last upgrade to reach for — not the first.
- **Not WADA-prohibited**; the real risk is contamination of a lightly regulated
  product.
