# Bone Health

Bone is the tissue people think about least and regret most. It feels like
scaffolding — inert, permanent, structural — but it's **living tissue** that is
constantly being torn down and rebuilt, and it responds to how you train and eat
exactly like muscle does. The difference is timing: you can't see or feel your bone
density changing, and the bill for neglecting it arrives decades later, as a fracture
that never fully heals.

The good news is that the two most powerful levers for bone are things this course
already champions: **lifting heavy things** and **eating enough**. The supplements
matter, but far less than the marketing implies.

## Bone is a living, loading-driven organ

Your skeleton is remodelled continuously by two cell crews: **osteoblasts** that
build bone and **osteoclasts** that break it down. Whether you gain or lose bone
depends on the balance between them — and that balance is set largely by **mechanical
load**.

This is **Wolff's law** in practice: bone adapts to the forces placed on it. Load it,
and the building crew wins. Stop loading it — bed rest, spaceflight, a limb in a cast,
or simply a sedentary life — and the demolition crew wins, fast.

The trajectory across a life:

- You **build bone through childhood and adolescence**, reaching **peak bone mass**
  in your late 20s. The bone you bank then is bone you get to spend later.
- You **hold roughly steady** through midlife.
- You **lose bone with age**, and the loss accelerates sharply in women at
  **menopause** when oestrogen — a major brake on bone breakdown — falls.

The goal at every age is the same: **build as much as you can while you can, and lose
as little as possible later.** Both are trainable.

## Loading is the primary stimulus — and supplements can't replace it

If bone health had a headline, it's this: **no amount of calcium or vitamin D builds
bone without the mechanical signal to use it.** Nutrients are raw materials; load is
the instruction to lay them down.

What bone responds to:

- **High-magnitude force** — heavy resistance training. Squats, deadlifts, presses,
  and loaded carries put real strain through the spine and hips, the fracture sites
  that matter most.
- **Impact and rapid loading** — jumping, hopping, running, and change-of-direction
  sport. Osteogenic loading likes **novel, high, fast** forces more than long, steady,
  low ones.
- **Progressive overload** — the same principle as muscle (see the training chapters).
  Bone adapts to loads it isn't used to, then needs more.

What bone largely ignores: **swimming and cycling**. Excellent for the heart and
lungs, they're **low-impact and non-weight-bearing**, so they do little for bone
density — a genuine blind spot for dedicated cyclists and swimmers, who can have
strong hearts and surprisingly weak hips. If that's you, add resistance and impact
work deliberately.

Resistance training is also why bone is a lifelong project rather than a young
person's game: even in **older adults**, heavy strength training and impact work
**maintain and can improve** bone density, while also cutting fall risk — and it's
falls that turn low bone density into a broken hip.

## The nutrients that actually matter

Loading tells bone to build; nutrition supplies what it builds with. The short list:

**Calcium** — the main mineral in bone. The **Nordic Nutrition Recommendations 2023**
put adult needs around **800–1000 mg/day**. Food first: dairy is the densest Nordic
source, but **fortified plant milks, tofu set with calcium, tinned fish with bones
(sardines, canned salmon), leafy greens, and almonds** all contribute. More is not
better — very high supplemental calcium doesn't strengthen bone further and may carry
downsides; aim to meet the target, mostly from food.

**Vitamin D** — required to absorb calcium from the gut, which is why it's the pair's
limiting factor. At Sweden's latitude the skin makes almost none from October to
March, so **shortfall is close to universal in winter** (see the micronutrients
chapter). Livsmedelsverket advises a supplement for much of the population in winter,
and for year-round risk groups (little sun exposure, darker skin, over-75s, covering
clothing). Test 25(OH)D if unsure; **~800–1000 IU/day** is a sensible maintenance
dose, more to correct a measured deficiency.

**Protein** — long feared as bad for bone (the "acid" myth); the modern evidence is
the opposite. Adequate protein is **associated with better bone density and fewer
fractures**, and it's essential for the collagen scaffold bone is built on. The
protein targets elsewhere in this course support your skeleton, not just your muscle.

**Vitamin K2** — helps direct calcium into bone (and away from arteries) by activating
proteins like osteocalcin. The evidence is **suggestive rather than conclusive**;
it's plausibly useful, cheap, and low-risk, but it's a minor player next to load,
calcium, and vitamin D. Found in fermented foods and some cheeses; a supplement is
optional, not essential.

**The supporting cast** — magnesium, phosphorus, and adequate overall energy all
play roles, but they're rarely the limiting factor in someone eating enough.

## The things that quietly erode bone

- **Under-eating (low energy availability).** The single biggest reversible threat in
  active people — covered next.
- **Smoking** and **heavy alcohol** both accelerate bone loss.
- **Very low body fat plus hard training**, especially in women, when it disrupts
  hormones (below).
- **Long-term inactivity and sitting** — bone follows the "use it or lose it" rule
  strictly.
- **Some medications** (notably long-term corticosteroids) — a medical conversation,
  not a supplement one.

## The danger windows: RED-S and menopause

Two situations turn slow background loss into a genuine problem, and both deserve
naming.

**RED-S / low energy availability.** When you don't eat enough to cover training, the
body cuts "optional" spending — and reproductive hormones, which protect bone, are
first on the chopping block. In women this shows up as a **disrupted or absent
menstrual cycle**; in men, as suppressed testosterone. The result is **accelerated
bone loss and stress fractures in young athletes** — the opposite of what training is
supposed to do. This is the mechanism behind the "female athlete triad," now
understood to affect all sexes as **RED-S** (Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport).
The fix is not more calcium — it's **eating enough**. A young endurance athlete with
a stress fracture and a missing period doesn't have a calcium problem; they have an
energy problem (see the special-populations and disordered-eating chapters).

**Menopause.** The fall in oestrogen removes a major brake on bone breakdown, and
women can lose bone **rapidly in the years around menopause** — which is why
osteoporosis and fragility fractures are far more common in women later in life. The
countermeasures are the same, applied deliberately and early: **resistance and impact
training, adequate protein, calcium and vitamin D**, and, for some women, medical
options (including hormone therapy) worth discussing with a doctor.

## Testing: DXA, osteopenia and osteoporosis

Bone density is measured by a **DXA scan**, reported as a **T-score** (how you compare
to a healthy young adult):

- **Normal:** T-score above **−1.0**
- **Osteopenia** (low bone mass): **−1.0 to −2.5**
- **Osteoporosis:** **−2.5 or below**

You don't need a scan in your 30s if you're healthy and training. It becomes relevant
with **risk factors**: a fragility fracture, early menopause, a history of RED-S or an
eating disorder, long-term steroid use, a strong family history, or being an older
adult. Sweden also uses the **FRAX** tool to estimate 10-year fracture risk and guide
who needs treatment. If you're in a risk group, ask at your **vårdcentral** — bone
loss is silent, so it's found by testing, not symptoms.

## A practical bone-health protocol

1. **Lift heavy, at least twice a week** — compound movements that load the spine and
   hips. This is the non-negotiable one.
2. **Add impact** if your joints tolerate it — jumps, hops, running, sport.
   Especially important if your cardio is swimming or cycling.
3. **Eat enough, full stop.** Chronic under-eating undoes everything else. Keep a
   regular menstrual cycle (women) as a free biofeedback signal.
4. **Hit ~800–1000 mg calcium/day, mostly from food**, and **enough protein**.
5. **Supplement vitamin D in winter** (and year-round if you're a risk group); test
   if unsure.
6. **Don't smoke; keep alcohol modest.**
7. **Get a DXA / FRAX assessment** if you have risk factors, particularly around
   menopause or after a low-trauma fracture.

## The takeaway

- Bone is **living tissue governed by load** — Wolff's law. **Heavy resistance
  training and impact win; supplements alone can't build bone.**
- You **bank peak bone mass by your late 20s**, hold it in midlife, and lose it with
  age — fastest in women at **menopause**. Build early, protect later.
- **Swimming and cycling do little for bone** — non-impact. Cyclists and swimmers
  need to add loading deliberately.
- Nutrients that matter: **calcium (~800–1000 mg/day, food first), vitamin D
  (supplement in the Swedish winter), and adequate protein**. **K2** is a plausible
  minor extra.
- The two danger windows are **RED-S / under-eating** (stress fractures in young
  athletes — the fix is eating enough, not calcium) and **menopause**.
- Bone loss is **silent**; if you have risk factors, get a **DXA scan / FRAX**
  assessment through your vårdcentral.
