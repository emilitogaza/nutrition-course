# Nutrition for Longevity & Healthspan

Longevity is having a moment. There are clinics, podcasts, supplement stacks, and a
great deal of money chasing the promise of extra years. Most of it is noise built on
mouse studies and wishful extrapolation. Underneath the noise, though, the picture
is genuinely encouraging — because the things that most reliably extend a healthy
life are the things this course has been about the whole time.

The key distinction is between **lifespan** (how long you live) and **healthspan**
(how long you live *well* — mobile, sharp, independent, off medications). Adding years
of frailty is a poor prize. The goal is to **compress the frail period** at the end —
to stay capable right up until you're not. Nutrition and training are the strongest
tools we have for that, and they work by protecting two things: **muscle** and
**fitness**.

## Muscle is a longevity organ

The most underrated fact in this field: **how much muscle you have and how strong you
are predict how long and how well you live.**

- **Sarcopenia** — the age-related loss of muscle — starts in your 30s and accelerates
  after 60, and it's a direct path to **frailty, falls, fractures, loss of
  independence, and death**. The hip fracture that ends an older person's independence
  usually starts as a fall, and the fall usually starts as weakness.
- **Grip strength and muscle mass** are among the better predictors of all-cause
  mortality in large studies — not because strong hands matter, but because they
  index whole-body robustness.
- Muscle is also a **metabolic sink**: it's where you dispose of blood glucose, so
  more muscle means better insulin sensitivity and lower diabetes risk (see the
  metabolic and heart chapters).

The implication reframes everything about "eating for old age." The priority isn't a
special anti-ageing diet — it's **arriving at old age with as much muscle and strength
as possible, and defending it.** Resistance training plus adequate protein is, in the
most literal sense, longevity medicine. **Creatine** earns a specific mention here:
its largest benefits show up in older adults training against sarcopenia (see the
creatine chapter).

## Fitness is the other longevity drug

The second pillar is **cardiorespiratory fitness**. As the VO₂ max chapter covered,
low fitness is associated with mortality risk on the scale of major diseases — and
unlike your genes, fitness is trainable at any age. The combination is what you want:

- **A strong aerobic engine** (fitness) for the heart, brain, and metabolism.
- **Strength and muscle** (resistance) for structure, metabolism, and independence.

Neither substitutes for the other. The longest, healthiest lives cluster in people who
have both.

## The protein-and-longevity debate, resolved by age

You may have run into a scary-sounding claim: that **high protein shortens life** by
driving the growth pathway **mTOR** and suppressing **autophagy** (the cellular
"clean-up" process), or that **IGF-1** from protein promotes cancer. This deserves a
straight answer, because it's caused real confusion.

The resolution is **age-dependent**, and it dissolves most of the apparent conflict:

- The alarming data comes mostly from **animal studies** (where lifelong protein
  restriction extends lifespan in mice — an organism whose entire life history differs
  from ours) and from **observational human data in middle age**, where the "high
  protein, higher risk" signal is heavily confounded by *what* the protein came from
  (processed and red meat travels with a lot else).
- In **older adults**, the relationship **flips**: higher protein is associated with
  **better** outcomes, because the dominant threat has changed. In your 70s and 80s,
  the danger isn't too much growth signalling — it's **not building enough muscle to
  stay out of a wheelchair**. Older muscle is also **anabolically resistant** (it
  needs more protein per meal to respond), so the elderly need *more* protein, not
  less.

The honest synthesis: for anyone training, and for anyone over ~65, **the risk of too
little protein (sarcopenia, frailty) vastly outweighs the speculative risk of too
much.** The mTOR-versus-autophagy framing treats a real cellular trade-off as if it
were a simple dietary dial — it isn't, and you don't manage it by eating less protein.

## The fasting question

The same growth-versus-repair logic drives the enthusiasm for **fasting and caloric
restriction**. What's real and what isn't:

- **Caloric restriction extends lifespan in animals** — one of the most reproducible
  findings in ageing research. In humans, sustained caloric restriction improves
  metabolic markers, but whether it extends human *lifespan* is unproven and
  practically very hard to sustain.
- **Time-restricted eating and intermittent fasting** (covered in the new-research
  chapter) work mainly through **calorie control and metabolic health**, not through
  a unique longevity switch. For someone training, an aggressive fasting window can
  **undermine muscle** by making protein hard to distribute — the exact opposite of
  the muscle-preservation priority above.
- **Autophagy** does increase with fasting, but the leap from "autophagy went up on a
  fast" to "therefore fasting makes humans live longer" is unsupported. Exercise also
  drives autophagy, without the muscle downside.

Verdict: fasting is a **legitimate tool for managing intake and metabolic health** if
it suits you — not a longevity cheat code, and worth being careful with if you're
trying to hold onto muscle.

## What the longest-lived populations actually eat

Strip away the supplement stacks and the pattern in genuinely long-lived, healthy
populations is unglamorous and consistent — and it matches the fibre, heart, and
diet-frameworks chapters almost point for point:

- **Mostly plants, lots of fibre and legumes**, plenty of variety.
- **Fish and modest amounts of meat**, more unsaturated than saturated fat (the
  Nordic and Mediterranean patterns both fit).
- **Not overeating** — staying lean, avoiding chronic energy surplus and the visceral
  fat that drives cardiometabolic disease.
- **Alcohol modest or absent** — the old "wine is protective" story has weakened
  considerably; less is better.
- Embedded in **daily movement, strong social ties, and sleep** — which brings us to
  the point.

## The supplements: mostly hype, a few plausible

The longevity supplement industry is enormous and mostly evidence-free in humans:

- **NMN, NR (NAD⁺ boosters), resveratrol, urolithin A, spermidine, rapamycin,
  metformin-for-healthy-people** — all have interesting mechanisms and mouse or
  early-phase data, and **none** has shown it extends healthy human life. Fascinating
  to watch; not worth building a routine around (apply the skeptic's checklist from
  the new-research chapter).
- The supplements with an actual case are the boring ones already covered:
  **creatine** (muscle and brain in ageing), **vitamin D** if you're low, **omega-3**,
  and **protein** to hit your targets. That's close to the whole defensible list.

## The unglamorous protocol that actually works

If you want to maximise healthspan, the evidence points somewhere deeply unsexy:

1. **Resistance-train for life.** Defend muscle and strength — the single highest-
   leverage anti-ageing intervention. Never stop lifting.
2. **Build and keep aerobic fitness.** The other longevity drug.
3. **Eat enough protein, and more as you age** (older muscle needs more per meal).
4. **Eat the pattern:** plants, fibre, legumes, fish, unsaturated fat; stay lean
   without chronic under- or over-eating.
5. **Protect sleep** (a body-composition *and* longevity variable — see the sleep
   chapter).
6. **Keep alcohol low, don't smoke, stay socially connected.**
7. **Get your numbers checked** so problems are caught early — the next chapter.

None of this requires a clinic or a supplement stack. It requires doing the ordinary
things for decades — which is exactly why the habits chapter matters as much as any
of the science.

## The takeaway

- Aim for **healthspan**, not just lifespan — compress the frail years by staying
  strong and fit to the end.
- **Muscle is a longevity organ:** strength and muscle mass predict how long and how
  well you live, and **sarcopenia** is a direct route to frailty. Defending muscle is
  the highest-leverage intervention.
- **Cardiorespiratory fitness is the other longevity drug** — trainable at any age,
  and you want both pillars.
- The **protein-shortens-life** scare is resolved by age: in the old (and in anyone
  training), the risk of **too little** protein dwarfs the speculative risk of too
  much. Older adults need **more**, not less.
- **Fasting** works via calorie control and metabolic health, not a magic longevity
  switch — and can undercut muscle if aggressive.
- **Longevity supplements (NMN, resveratrol, etc.) are unproven in humans.** The real
  list is the boring one: lift, train aerobically, eat protein and a plant-rich
  pattern, sleep, stay lean, don't smoke.
