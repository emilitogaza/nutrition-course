# Supplements That Don't (or Barely) Work

The supplement industry is enormous, loosely regulated, and built on hope. For
every creatine there are dozens of products whose main active ingredient is
**marketing**. Knowing what *not* to buy saves money and protects you from
genuine risk. None of the below is a blanket "never" — but each is oversold
relative to its evidence.

## BCAAs

Covered in the amino-acids chapter, but they belong here too: **branched-chain
amino acids** were sold on the leucine/MPS story while omitting the other six
essential amino acids needed to actually build tissue. If you eat enough total
protein, they add **nothing**. Whole protein or full EAAs are strictly better.
One of the most profitable near-useless products in the category.

## "Testosterone boosters"

Tribulus, D-aspartic acid, fenugreek, ashwagandha blends, ZMA, and the rest.

- In men with **normal hormones**, these produce **little to no** meaningful rise
  in testosterone or performance in controlled trials.
- Where an ingredient *does* help, it's usually correcting a **deficiency** (e.g.
  zinc or vitamin D if you were low) — which a targeted approach handles better.
- **Ashwagandha** has some evidence for stress/cortisol and sleep, which can
  indirectly help — but it's not a testosterone drug.

If testosterone is genuinely low, that's a **medical** issue, not a supplement
one.

## Fat burners

Thermogenic "fat burners" are mostly **caffeine plus a stimulant stack** and a
long list of ingredients at doses too low to matter. The caffeine gives a real
but small metabolic bump and appetite blunting; the rest is filler. They do
**not** meaningfully "melt fat" — an energy deficit does. Some stimulant blends
carry real cardiovascular risk. **Green tea extract, L-carnitine, CLA,
raspberry ketones, garcinia** — all range from trivial to useless for fat loss.

## Glutamine

A popular recovery/immune supplement. Glutamine is genuinely important
physiologically — but the body makes plenty, and in healthy, well-fed athletes,
supplementing it does **little for muscle, recovery, or immunity**. (It has real
clinical uses in critically ill or gut-compromised patients — a different
context.)

## Berberine — "nature's Ozempic"

The label that made it go viral is also the fastest way to understand it: berberine
is **not** nature's Ozempic, and the comparison is marketing built on a category
error. Berberine is a plant alkaloid that activates **AMPK**, and it does have real
metabolic effects — meta-analyses show it **lowers fasting glucose, HbA1c, and LDL**
by amounts roughly comparable to older glucose-lowering drugs like metformin in
people with **type 2 diabetes or metabolic syndrome**.

That is a genuinely different claim from "it makes you lose weight like a GLP-1":

- The weight-loss effect in trials is **small** — a couple of kilos at most, nothing
  like the 15–25% seen with the actual drugs (see the GLP-1 chapter).
- Almost all the good data is in **metabolically unwell** populations. In healthy,
  lean, training people the effect on body composition is unproven and probably
  trivial.
- **Bioavailability is famously poor** (under ~1%), so what a random capsule
  delivers is anyone's guess — and supplements aren't tested for content.
- It's an **AMPK activator**, and AMPK is the pathway that opposes mTOR. There's a
  reasonable theoretical concern that chronic use could **blunt the muscle-building
  signal** from training — the last thing you want around lifting.
- It **interacts with medications** (it inhibits the CYP enzymes that clear many
  drugs) and shouldn't be combined casually, especially in pregnancy.

If you have a diagnosed metabolic condition, it's a real compound worth discussing
with a doctor — not a shortcut to abs.

## Ashwagandha & the adaptogens

Ashwagandha (*Withania somnifera*) is the one herb in this chapter with something
real behind it — just not the thing it's usually sold for. It gets marketed as a
**testosterone booster**; it's better understood as a **stress and sleep** agent.

- The **best-supported** effect is on **perceived stress and cortisol** — several
  RCTs show modest reductions in stress scores and evening cortisol, and some show
  improved sleep quality. That's a legitimate, if moderate, effect.
- The **testosterone** and **strength/muscle** claims rest on a handful of small,
  often industry-funded trials with sizeable effect sizes that **haven't replicated**
  cleanly. Treat "ashwagandha builds muscle" as unproven.
- Because its plausible benefit is **downstream of better sleep and lower stress**,
  it competes with — and rarely beats — actually sleeping more (see the sleep and
  recovery chapter).
- Caveats that matter: rare reports of **liver injury**, it can nudge **thyroid
  hormones up**, and it's **not for pregnancy**. Standardised extracts (e.g.
  KSM-66, Sensoril) at least tell you the dose.

The broader **"adaptogen"** category — rhodiola, ginseng, cordyceps, tongkat — is a
marketing bucket, not a pharmacological one. Rhodiola has weak evidence for fatigue;
the rest is thin.

## Tongkat ali, fadogia & turkesterone — the "natural test" revival

The testosterone-booster genre never dies; it just rotates ingredients. The current
crop:

- **Tongkat ali (*Eurycoma longifolia*)** — the strongest of a weak field. Small
  trials suggest it may help **stress and, in some studies, testosterone in
  hypogonadal or stressed men**. Data in healthy young lifters is sparse; contents
  vary wildly between products.
- **Fadogia agrestis** — popularised almost entirely by a podcast, not by human
  data. The evidence is **rodent-only**, and the rat studies raise a **testicular
  toxicity** signal at higher doses. There are essentially **no human safety or
  efficacy studies**. That combination — no human evidence, a toxicity flag — makes
  it one to avoid, not experiment with.
- **Turkesterone** — an ecdysteroid sold as a "natural anabolic." Human evidence is
  effectively **nonexistent**, and independent testing has repeatedly found products
  **contain little or none** of what's on the label.

The pattern is always the same: a compelling mechanism, a rodent study or a
charismatic advocate, and no controlled human trials showing a meaningful effect at
a dose you can verify is actually in the bottle.

## "Detox," "cleanses," and greens powders

- **Detox teas and cleanses** — your liver and kidneys already detoxify you;
  these products mostly deliver laxatives and lost water weight. No credible
  evidence of benefit, occasional real harm.
- **Greens powders** — not harmful, but an expensive, unstandardised way to
  half-replace vegetables. If you eat vegetables, you don't need them; if you
  don't, a proper multivitamin is cheaper and better characterised.

## The regulatory reality

In most countries (including under the US **DSHEA** framework), supplements are
**not** pre-approved for safety or efficacy the way drugs are. Manufacturers can
sell first and get challenged later. That means:

- **Label claims may not match contents** — doses off, ingredients missing, or
  undeclared additives present.
- **Proprietary blends** hide how little of each active ingredient you're getting.
- **Contamination** with banned or dangerous substances is documented and
  recurring.

## How to evaluate any new supplement

A quick filter before you spend:

1. **Is there a plausible mechanism** *and* human trials in trained people — not
   just a cell/rodent study or a mechanism story?
2. **What's the effect size** — is it big enough to notice, or just
   "statistically significant"?
3. **Is the dose in the product** the same as the dose that was studied?
4. **Who funded the studies**, and is it third-party tested?

If a product can't clear these, it's almost certainly the marketing talking.

## The takeaway

- **BCAAs, test boosters, fat burners, glutamine, detoxes, and greens powders**
  are oversold relative to their evidence.
- **Berberine** has real effects on glucose and LDL *in metabolically unwell
  people*, but it's **not** "nature's Ozempic" — the weight effect is small and it
  may even oppose the muscle-building signal.
- **Ashwagandha** is a modest **stress/sleep** aid, not a testosterone drug.
- **Tongkat ali** is the least-bad "natural test" herb; **fadogia** is rodent-only
  with a toxicity signal, and **turkesterone** is unproven and routinely
  underdosed — avoid the last two.
- Fat loss comes from an **energy deficit**, not a pill.
- Supplements are **lightly regulated** — claims, doses, and purity aren't
  guaranteed.
- Filter every product by **mechanism + human evidence + correct dose + third-
  party testing**.
